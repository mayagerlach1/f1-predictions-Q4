How It Works

Step 1: score every driver. Five factors are calculated from real data, each scaled so the best driver scores 100:

Season results

Points per race started, including sprints. Rewards consistency across the whole year.

Car pace

Every finish by both team cars in the last 8 races. A great car lifts both drivers.

Recent form

The driver's last 6 races, weighted 6-5-4-3-2-1 so the newest counts most. Retirements are left out here and handled by retire risk instead.

Qualifying speed

Share of 2026 qualifying sessions where the driver reached Q3. Raw single-lap speed matters because overtaking is hard.

Mexico history

Finishes here in 2025 (50%), 2024 (30%) and 2023 (20%). A retirement counts as 20th. Drivers who never raced here get a neutral score.

Retire risk

Each driver's real retirement rate in 2026, blended with the grid average so one unlucky weekend doesn't dominate.

Step 2: combine them. The sliders set each factor's weight. The default weights came from testing the model on rounds 7–18 and picking what predicted the real winners best. Season results and car pace mattered most; recent form added surprisingly little.

Step 3: add qualifying. In 2026 the pole-sitter won 12 of the 16 races where the pole is confirmed. Once you enter Saturday's top 3, the pole-sitter gets a large boost (tuned so pole-sitters win about 57% of the time — a bit cautious given the small sample), with smaller boosts for P2 and P3.

Step 4: simulate. The race is run 40,000 times. Each time, every driver may retire based on their retire risk, and random "race luck" is added to their strength. Whoever comes out on top wins that run. A driver's win chance is the share of runs they won.

Limits. The model doesn't know about car upgrades, tyre strategy, weather, penalties, or practice pace. One race is very random: even the best F1 models are wrong a lot. Treat these as well-informed odds, not a guarantee.
